## Section: Abstract

### Forecasting efficacy
**Definition:** how good a forecasting method is — its accuracy and usefulness for the decision at hand.
**Plain English:** "does the prediction actually work?" — measured by how close predictions land to
reality. **Example:** two methods predict next-quarter purchases; the one whose predictions are
consistently closer to what customers actually did has higher forecasting efficacy.

### Pareto
**Definition:** here, a **probability distribution** used to describe how long customers stay active
before silently quitting — *not* the 80/20 rule. **Plain English:** the "when do they quit?" curve: many
customers drift away early, a few stay loyal for a very long time. **Example:** across a customer base,
lifetimes might range from weeks to years; the Pareto curve says how common each lifetime length is.
*(It arises from mixing exponential lifetimes across customers with a Gamma spread — see below.)*

### Negative binomial distribution (NBD)
**Definition:** a probability distribution for **counts** (0, 1, 2, …) that is more spread out than a
plain Poisson because the underlying rate varies across individuals. **Plain English:** the "how often do
they buy?" curve — flexible enough that some people buy constantly and others rarely. **Example:** the
number of orders customers place in a year — a few place dozens, most place a handful; the NBD fits that
lumpy shape. *(Formally: Poisson buying + Gamma-distributed rates across people = NBD.)*

### Pareto/NBD (and "Pareto/NBD model-based forecasts")
**Definition:** the classic buy-till-you-die model = **NBD** purchase process (how often, while alive) +
**Pareto** dropout process (when they quit); "model-based forecasts" are predictions produced by fitting
this model to data. **Plain English:** glue the "how often they buy" curve to the "when they quit" curve
and you can forecast each customer's future. **Example:** feeding a customer's purchase dates into a
fitted Pareto/NBD to predict their next-6-months order count. **Contrast:** *model-based* (uses the
fitted model) vs. *heuristic* (a rule of thumb, below).

```
   Pareto/NBD = [ NBD: how often they buy while alive ] + [ Pareto: when they silently quit ]
                        (purchase process)                        (dropout process)
```

### Model-based forecasts
**Definition:** predictions generated by a fitted statistical model rather than a rule of thumb.
**Plain English:** let the math weigh each customer's full history instead of guessing. **Example:** "the
model estimates this customer's buying rate and quit-risk, then predicts 3 purchases" — vs. a heuristic's
"they bought 3 last period, so guess 3."

### Monte Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) / MCMC-based estimates
**Definition:** a Bayesian fitting method that draws many samples of the parameters from their *posterior*
by taking a cleverly designed random walk through parameter space; the collected draws are the estimate.
**Plain English:** instead of one best guess, MCMC produces a whole *cloud* of plausible parameter values,
visiting each in proportion to how well it fits. **Example:** fitting the Pareto/NBD with MCMC yields
thousands of $(r,\alpha,s,\beta)$ draws; their median is your estimate and their spread is your
uncertainty. *(Note: "Markov Chain Monte Carlo" is the usual ordering; Simon writes "Monte Carlo Markov
Chain" — same thing.)*

### Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)
**Definition:** a fitting method that picks the single parameter setting making the observed data most
probable (the **mode** of the likelihood). **Plain English:** "find the peak" — the one dial-setting that
best explains what you saw. **Example:** MLE returns one number for each of the four Pareto/NBD parameters,
with no error bars attached (you'd add those via bootstrapping). *(See [doc 1, Q2](01_why_paretonbd_is_hard_and_bgnbd_is_easy.md)
for what a likelihood is.)*

### Heuristics
**Definition:** simple rules of thumb that approximate a forecast without fitting a probability model.
**Plain English:** a quick shortcut — cheap, no model, often naive. **Example:** "assume each customer
keeps buying at their past average rate for the rest of the horizon." Simon finds heuristics lose to the
fitted model on three of the four tasks.

### Confidence intervals
**Definition:** a range that expresses uncertainty around a forecast — e.g. a 90% interval is meant to
contain the true value 90% of the time. **Plain English:** not just "we predict 4" but "we predict 4, and
we're fairly sure it's between 2 and 7." **Example:** MCMC gives an interval directly from its cloud of
draws; MLE gets one via bootstrapping. **Why it matters:** a point forecast with no interval hides how
uncertain it is.

### Customer base analysis (CBA)
**Definition:** the study of a company's customers *as a group* to predict future behavior — who will buy,
how often, how much, who will churn. **Plain English:** using past transaction records to forecast the
whole customer base's future. **Example:** an e-commerce firm analyzing its order history to project next
year's active customers and revenue.

## Section: 1. Introduction

### Buy-till-you-die (BTYD)
**Definition:** a family of models in which a customer buys at some rate while "alive," then at a hidden
moment becomes permanently inactive ("dies"). **Plain English:** people keep shopping until they silently
quit for good — and you never see the quit. **Example:** the Pareto/NBD and BG/NBD are both BTYD models.

### Non-contractual setting (of CBA)
**Definition:** a business relationship with **no explicit end signal** — customers just stop buying,
there's no cancellation. **Plain English:** retail, groceries, e-commerce — nobody tells you they've left.
**Contrast:** *contractual* (gym, Netflix) where quitting is an observed event. **Example:** a supermarket
can't see churn; a mobile carrier (contract) can. This is why dropout must be *inferred*.

### Dropout / unobserved dropout
**Definition:** the moment a customer becomes permanently inactive; "unobserved" because in a
non-contractual setting it leaves no trace in the data. **Plain English:** the silent, invisible quit —
you can only guess it happened from a growing gap since the last purchase. **Example:** a shopper's last
order was 10 months ago — did they drop out, or are they just slow? You can't see which.

### Purchase pattern of a customer
**Definition:** the sequence and timing of a customer's transactions over time. **Plain English:** their
personal buying rhythm — how often and when they order. **Example:** "bought in Jan, Feb, Feb, May, then
nothing" is one customer's purchase pattern.

### Distribution
**Definition:** a mathematical description of how likely each possible value of a quantity is. **Plain
English:** a curve showing which outcomes are common and which are rare. **Example:** the distribution of
customer ages might peak at 30–40 and thin out toward 80. *(All the named curves below — Pareto, NBD,
Gamma, exponential, Beta — are distributions.)*

### Distribution of the dropout time
**Definition:** the curve describing when customers quit — how probable each possible lifetime length is.
**Plain English:** "of all customers, how many quit after 1 month, 6 months, 3 years?" **Example:** in the
Pareto/NBD this is governed by the Pareto (exponential lifetime + Gamma spread).

### Beta-geometric dropout process
**Definition:** a *discrete* dropout mechanism in which a customer quits with some probability **after
each purchase** (geometric), and that probability varies across people via a **Beta** distribution.
**Plain English:** flip a personal coin after every purchase — heads, you're done; the coin's bias differs
per customer. **Example:** it's the dropout process of the BG/NBD. *(Full treatment:
[doc 1, Part 2](01_why_paretonbd_is_hard_and_bgnbd_is_easy.md).)*

### (Modified) beta-geometric dropout process — *what is "modified" and why*
**Definition:** the MBG/NBD variant adds a chance to drop out **at time zero** (before any repeat
purchase), on top of the after-each-purchase dropout. **Plain English:** it fixes a bug: in the plain
BG/NBD, quitting requires a purchase, so a customer who *never repeats* is forced to be "100% alive" —
obviously wrong. The modification lets one-and-done customers be counted as gone. **Example:** someone who
bought once and vanished: plain BG/NBD says $P(\text{alive})=1$; MBG/NBD can say they've likely dropped
out. **Why:** to repair the one-time-buyer quirk (Batislam 2007; Hoppe–Wagner 2007).

### Low-threshold use of model extensions
**Definition:** extensions (like covariates) that are now cheap and easy to apply thanks to modern
computing. **Plain English:** "low effort to adopt" — you no longer need a research project to add
features. **Example:** bolting region and email-exposure onto a Pareto/NBD is now routine. *(Slightly
non-native phrasing; read "low-threshold" as "easy to reach for.")*

### Static and time-variant covariates
**Definition:** extra explanatory variables about a customer; **static** = fixed over time, **time-variant**
= changing over time. **Plain English:** side-information that helps predict buying — some of it constant,
some of it moving. **Example:** *static* = acquisition channel, sign-up cohort; *time-variant* = this
month's promotions received, seasonality.

### Gamma distributed / Gamma distribution with parameters r and α
**Definition:** a flexible distribution for **positive** quantities (like rates), with shape $r$ and scale
$\alpha$. **Plain English:** the go-to curve for "a positive number that varies across people," able to be
skewed or bell-like. **Example:** in the Pareto/NBD, customers' individual **buying rates** are Gamma$(r,\alpha)$
across the base — most buy slowly, a few buy fast. **Role:** it's the "spread across customers" (mixing)
curve that turns one person's Poisson into a population NBD.

### Exponentially distributed / exponential inter-purchase times / with parameter λᵢ
**Definition:** the exponential distribution describes waiting times when events happen at a constant rate
$\lambda$; it is **memoryless** (the expected wait-to-next doesn't shrink as you wait). **Plain English:**
a customer buying "at rate $\lambda_i$" has gaps between purchases that are exponential — and how long
they've already waited tells you nothing about when the next one comes. **Example:** if $\lambda_i$
implies ~40-day average gaps, a 5-day gap and a 120-day gap are both unremarkable. **Consequence:** this
memorylessness is *why the timing forecast fails* (see [doc 2](02_simon2025_abstract_quote_by_quote.md)).

### Inter-purchase times (IPT)
**Definition:** the time gaps between a customer's consecutive purchases. **Plain English:** "how long
between orders." **Example:** orders on day 0, 30, 75 → IPTs of 30 and 45 days. In the Pareto/NBD these
are exponential; in the Pareto/GGG they're the more flexible **Gamma** (allowing *regular* buyers).

### Cohorts / simultaneously analysing different cohorts
**Definition:** a **cohort** is a group of customers grouped by a shared start (e.g. everyone acquired in
Q1 2020); analyzing several at once means fitting/comparing across these groups. **Plain English:**
customers who "joined together," studied side by side. **Example:** our project uses seven cohorts (CDNow,
Online Retail II, Dunnhumby, …) and compares model behavior across all of them.

### Likelihoods / closed-form likelihoods
**Definition:** the **likelihood** scores each parameter setting by how probable it makes the observed
data; a **closed-form** likelihood can be written as one finite formula (no infinite series / numerical
integral). **Plain English:** the fitting engine; "closed-form" means it's simple enough to compute
directly (even in a spreadsheet). **Example:** the BG/NBD likelihood is closed-form (Gamma/Beta functions);
the Pareto/NBD's needs a Gauss hypergeometric function and isn't. *(Full detail:
[doc 1, Q2–Q4](01_why_paretonbd_is_hard_and_bgnbd_is_easy.md).)*

### Parametric models / well-fitting / well-fitting parametric models
**Definition:** a **parametric model** has a fixed, pre-specified shape with a small set of parameters;
**well-fitting** means that pre-specified shape genuinely matches the data. **Plain English:** a model that
"already knows the shape" of behavior and only needs to learn a few numbers — and, when the shape is right,
fits well. **Example:** the 4-parameter Pareto/NBD. **Strength:** structure lets it work on thin data.
**Weakness:** if its assumed shape is wrong, no amount of data fixes it.

### Parsimonious data
**Definition:** sparse / thin data — few observations per customer, or few customers. **Plain English:**
"not much to go on." **Example:** a base where most customers made only one or two purchases. **Why it
matters:** parametric models cope with parsimonious data *because* their structure fills the gaps; ML
struggles here (it needs volume).

### Bias / cohort level / lower bias at the cohort level
**Definition:** **bias** = systematic error (consistently over- or under-shooting the truth); **cohort
level** = evaluated for a whole group rather than per individual; so "lower bias at the cohort level" =
the group-total prediction is systematically closer to the truth. **Plain English:** individual guesses
can be noisy, but do they *add up* to the right total for the cohort? **Example:** a model might mis-rank
individuals yet nail the cohort's *total* purchases — low cohort-level bias. **Why:** individual errors can
cancel when summed, so aggregate forecasts are often trustworthy even when individual ones aren't.

### Calibration length
**Definition:** the length of the **past** period used to fit (calibrate) the model. **Plain English:**
"how much history you train on." **Example:** fit on the first 39 weeks (calibration), forecast the next
13 (forecast period). **Careful:** here "calibration" means *the training window*, a different sense from
"calibration" as forecast honesty in our project — same word, two meanings.

### Forecast period
**Definition:** the **future** window over which predictions are made and later checked. **Plain English:**
"how far ahead you predict." **Example:** Simon uses 13, 26, and 52 weeks. Pairs with calibration length:
train on the past, predict the forecast period.

### Interpretable parameters
**Definition:** parameters that carry direct real-world meaning. **Plain English:** each dial *means*
something a manager can read. **Example:** the Pareto/NBD's $r,\alpha$ describe the buying-rate spread and
$s,\beta$ the dropout-rate spread — unlike a neural net's thousands of opaque weights. **Why it matters:**
interpretability aids trust and diagnosis.

### Point estimates / point estimates for the deviations measures
**Definition:** a **point estimate** is a single best-guess number (no interval); "for the deviations
measures" means reporting a single number for the *typical forecast error*. **Plain English:** "one number,
no error bars" — including one summary number for how wrong the forecasts tend to be. **Example:** "mean
absolute error = 2.1 purchases" is a point estimate of the deviation. **Contrast:** an interval or full
distribution conveys uncertainty; a point estimate doesn't.

### Bootstrapping / applying bootstrapping to MLE / parameter re-estimation
**Definition:** **bootstrapping** = resample the data with replacement many times, refit each time, and use
the spread of results as an uncertainty estimate; applied to MLE, each round is a quick **parameter
re-estimation**. **Plain English:** "shuffle-and-refit a few hundred times and watch how much the answer
wanders" — that wander is your error bar. **Example:** bootstrap the Pareto/NBD → get a confidence interval
for MLE, rivaling MCMC's. **Key efficiency:** re-fitting 4 parameters is cheap; bootstrapping ML (which
retrains a whole heavy model each round) is not.

### Unnormalised probability distribution
**Definition:** a function with the right *shape* of a probability distribution but not scaled to integrate
to 1. **Plain English:** the curve's *relative* heights are right, but it isn't rescaled to be a proper
probability. **Example:** the likelihood viewed over parameters — for finding its **peak**, the missing
scaling doesn't matter. **Why mentioned:** MLE only needs the peak, so it can ignore normalization.

### Mode (i.e., the parameter value(s) yielding the highest likelihood value)
**Definition:** the **peak** of a distribution — the single most probable value. **Plain English:** the top
of the hill. **Example:** MLE returns the mode of the likelihood — the dial-setting at its highest point.

```
        ▲ mode (MLE picks this one point)
       ╱ ╲
      ╱   ╲     ← the whole curve is what MCMC explores (mean / median / quantiles)
  ───╯     ╰───►  parameter value
```

### Posterior distribution
**Definition:** in Bayesian statistics, the distribution of the parameters *after* combining prior belief
with the observed data. **Plain English:** "what we believe the parameters are, given the data" — a whole
curve, not one point. **Example:** MCMC's cloud of draws approximates the posterior; you read off its
median as an estimate and its width as uncertainty.

### Mean, median, and quantiles
**Definition:** summaries of a distribution — **mean** (average), **median** (middle value), **quantiles**
(cut-points like the 5th/95th percentile). **Plain English:** ways to boil a cloud of values down to
headline numbers, including for error bars. **Example:** from MCMC draws: median = point estimate; 5th &
95th percentiles = a 90% interval. **Why:** MCMC gives all of these; MLE gives only a point.

### Heterogeneity parameters
**Definition:** the parameters that control how much customers **differ** from one another (the spread of
the mixing distributions). **Plain English:** the dials for "how varied is the customer base?" **Example:**
in the Pareto/NBD, $r,\alpha$ set how spread out buying rates are, and $s,\beta$ how spread out dropout
rates are. **Heterogeneity** = the fact that customers are not all alike; these parameters quantify it.

### Parameter recovery
**Definition:** a test on *simulated* data (where the true parameters are known) checking how well a
fitting method reconstructs them. **Plain English:** "if we know the answer, does the method find it?"
**Example:** simulate customers from known $(r,\alpha,s,\beta)$, fit, and see if you get those values back.
**Why:** good recovery is evidence the method is trustworthy on real data.

### Goodness-of-fit (of different models)
**Definition:** how well a model matches observed data overall; comparing it across models ranks which
fits best. **Plain English:** "which model explains the data best?" **Example:** comparing Pareto/NBD vs.
BG/NBD by how closely each reproduces the observed purchase histories. **Note:** Simon points out this is
how counts were *usually* used before — to rank models — rather than to measure error magnitude.

### Customer lifetime value (CLV)
**Definition:** the expected, discounted future profit from a customer over their remaining relationship.
**Plain English:** "what is this customer worth to us, in today's money?" **Example:** expected purchases ×
expected margin per purchase, discounted for time. **Link:** built on the *count* forecast (task 1) — which
is why CLV isn't a separate fifth forecast.

### Empirical data sets / empirical validation
**Definition:** **empirical data** = real-world data (actual customer records); **empirical validation** =
testing a method on such real data (not just simulations). **Plain English:** "does it work on real
customers, not just made-up ones?" **Example:** Simon validates on proprietary Gusto/Homeshopping data; our
project uses seven *public* cohorts for replicability.

### Parameter estimation / data-set-generating parameters
**Definition:** **parameter estimation** = the act of fitting a model's parameters to data; **data-set-generating
parameters** = the *true* parameters used to simulate a synthetic data set. **Plain English:** "learning the
dials from data" vs. "the dials we secretly used to create the test data." **Example:** in a recovery study
you *generate* data from known parameters, then *estimate* them back and compare.

## Section: 2. Forecasting methods

### Latent attrition process
**Definition:** the hidden ("latent") mechanism by which customers drop out; "attrition" = churn/loss.
**Plain English:** the invisible quitting process the model has to infer. **Example:** the Pareto lifetime
in the Pareto/NBD is a latent attrition process — never observed, only estimated. *(Synonym cluster:
dropout process, latent attrition, buy-till-you-**die**.)*

### Heterogeneous likelihood
**Definition:** a likelihood that accounts for customers being **different** by mixing individual
behaviors over the heterogeneity (Gamma/Beta) distributions. **Plain English:** the fitting engine, built
to respect that customers vary rather than assuming everyone is identical. **Example:** the Pareto/NBD
likelihood integrates each customer's Poisson/exponential behavior over the population Gamma spreads —
that mixing is what makes it "heterogeneous." *(This mixing over a continuous lifetime is exactly what
produces the hard hypergeometric function — [doc 1](01_why_paretonbd_is_hard_and_bgnbd_is_easy.md).)*

*(**Heterogeneity parameters** — repeated from the Introduction — are the $r,\alpha,s,\beta$ that set how
wide those spreads are; see above.)*

## Section: 2.1 Purchase forecast

### Simulated purchase patterns (SPP)
**Definition:** a forecasting routine that **forward-simulates** many possible future purchase paths for a
customer from the fitted (MCMC) parameters and summarizes them (e.g. their median). **Plain English:**
"play out the customer's future thousands of times and average" — a Monte Carlo forecast. **Example:** for
one customer, simulate 5,000 futures under the posterior draws; the median future count is the SPP point
forecast (and the spread gives an interval).

### Individual conditional expectation (ICE)
**Definition:** the **closed-form** expected number of future purchases for a customer, conditional on
their observed history — $E[x^{*}\mid \text{history}]$. **Plain English:** plug the customer's data into a
formula and read off their expected future count directly (no simulation). **Example:** given a customer's
frequency, recency, and age, the ICE formula returns "expected 2.7 purchases next 26 weeks." **Contrast
with SPP:** ICE = one formula (fast, a mean); SPP = many simulations (also gives the distribution).

### Heterogeneous formula
**Definition:** a forecast formula that already integrates over customer differences (the heterogeneity
distributions). **Plain English:** a single expression that "bakes in" the fact that customers vary.
**Example:** the ICE expectation is a heterogeneous formula — it's the population-mixed expected count, not
a one-size-fits-all guess.

### Mean purchase rate
**Definition:** the average number of purchases per unit time for a customer (or the population) while
active — the $\lambda$ of the Poisson buying process, or its population average $r/\alpha$. **Plain
English:** "how often, on average, do they buy while alive?" **Example:** a mean purchase rate of 0.1/week
≈ one purchase every 10 weeks. **Role:** combined with how long they stay alive, it drives the expected
future count.

## Quick map of how the big pieces fit

```
   Each customer, while ALIVE, buys at a Poisson rate λ          ← "how often"
        └─ λ varies across people as Gamma(r, α)                  ← heterogeneity (NBD)
   Each customer silently DROPS OUT at an exponential-lifetime μ  ← "when they quit"
        └─ μ varies across people as Gamma(s, β)                  ← heterogeneity (Pareto)
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   Fit (r,α,s,β) by:  MLE (the peak) · MCMC (the whole cloud) · heuristic (shortcut)
   Forecast:          counts · active(x*>0) · top-A% · timing        (the four tasks)
```
