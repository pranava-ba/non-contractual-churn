Start here

Your 60-second pitch

If the panel says "tell us about your project," say this. It contains every keyword on your deck in the right order.

In businesses like e-commerce, retail or gaming, a customer never tells you they've left — they just quietly stop buying. That's non-contractual churn, and unlike a Netflix cancellation there is no label that says who churned and when.

We predict it with Buy-Till-You-Die (BTYD) models. Each customer is two hidden processes: while "alive" they purchase at random moments (a Poisson process), and at some unseen time they "die" — drop out forever. Because different customers buy at different rates, the population's purchase counts follow a Negative Binomial Distribution — that's the "NBD."

From only each customer's Recency, Frequency and age on file (RFM + T), the model computes P(alive) — the probability they're still active — which is our churn score. A Gamma-Gamma model adds each customer's monetary value, giving Customer Lifetime Value (CLV). We benchmark these interpretable probability models against machine-learning baselines. The novelty: we use the model's P(alive) as a soft churn label to get around the missing labels, and we prioritise retention by customer value, not just churn risk.

The problem

01Contractual vs non-contractual — and why churn goes invisible

This is the framing your whole project rests on. Nail the distinction and the rest follows.

Contractual

Netflix, a gym, a phone plan. The customer signs up and cancels. You see the exact churn event and date. Churn is observed and labelled — ordinary supervised classification works.

Non-contractual

Amazon, a shop, a mobile game. Customers buy whenever they like with no subscription. Someone silent for 3 months might be gone — or just between purchases. Churn is latent (hidden) and must be inferred.

Why it's hard

(1) No churn label to train on. (2) Silence is ambiguous — a dormant customer looks identical to a dead one. (3) Purchases are irregular, so simple "days since last order" rules are noisy. BTYD models solve all three by modelling the invisible dropout instead of needing a label for it.

Building blocks

02RFM + T, and the two hidden processes

BTYD needs only a compact summary of each customer's history — no demographics, no labels.

The inputs: RFM + T

  • Recency — when the last purchase happened (the model uses t_x, the customer's age at their last transaction). Recent buyer → probably alive.
  • Frequency — how many repeat purchases, x. (Repeat only: the first purchase starts the clock.)
  • Monetary — average spend per transaction, m. Used later by Gamma-Gamma, not by the churn part.
  • T (age on file) — total time observed, from first purchase to the end of the study window.
The trio that matters

The churn model runs on just (x, t_x, T) — frequency, recency, and how long you've watched the customer. That's it. From these three numbers per customer it recovers everything.

"Buy Till You Die" = two processes

  • Transaction process (while alive): the customer makes purchases at random times at some personal average rate λ — a Poisson process.
  • Dropout process (death): at some unobserved moment the customer becomes permanently inactive. You never see this happen — you infer it from the silence that follows.

The statistical engine

03Why "Negative Binomial"?

This is the single most likely deep-dive question. Here's the clean answer.

One customer, while alive, buys at random at rate λ, so over a period their purchase count is Poisson(λ). But customers are not identical — some are heavy buyers (high λ), some light (low λ). We model that spread of rates with a Gamma distribution.

λ ~ Gamma(r, α) — spread of purchase rates across customers
count | λ ~ Poisson(λt) — one customer's buying
─────────── mix the two ───────────
count ~ Negative Binomial (NBD)

Mathematically, a Poisson whose rate is Gamma-distributed becomes a Negative Binomial. So the NBD is what you get when you allow every customer to have their own buying rate.

Why not just Poisson?

Real purchase data is overdispersed — its variance is much larger than its mean, because of that customer-to-customer variation. A single Poisson forces variance = mean and fits badly. The Negative Binomial has an extra parameter that absorbs the overdispersion. That is the whole reason "negative binomial" is in your project title.

The two models on your slides

Both share the NBD buying engine. They differ only in how they model death. Know this table cold.

The only real difference

Pareto/NBD lets death happen at any time; BG/NBD assumes you only "decide to leave" just after buying. That one simplification removes the hard integral and makes BG/NBD the go-to. Both output the same two things: P(alive) and expected future purchases.

From model to decision

05P(alive), Gamma-Gamma & CLV

The model outputs three things a business can act on.

1 · P(alive) — the churn score

Given a customer's (x, t_x, T), the model computes the probability they are still active now. Bought often and recently → high P(alive). A long silence after a burst of buying → low P(alive) → likely churned. No label needed — it's inferred from behaviour and the fitted model. This is your churn prediction.

2 · Gamma-Gamma — how much they spend

BTYD says how many future purchases; the Gamma-Gamma model says how much per purchase. It assumes each customer's spend varies around their own average, and those averages are Gamma-distributed across customers (hence the name). Key assumption: monetary value is independent of purchase frequency — a claim you can check (the correlation should be near zero).

3 · CLV — putting it together

CLV ≈ expected future transactions (BTYD) × expected value per transaction (Gamma-Gamma), discounted over time

Customer Lifetime Value is what a customer is worth going forward. It lets you rank customers, so retention effort goes to the high-value people who are also at risk — not just anyone likely to leave.

Fitting the parameters

06How you actually estimate the model

"Fitting" means finding r, α, and the dropout parameters from data. Three ways — and one of your papers (Simon, 2025) compares them.

MLE — Maximum Likelihood

Pick the parameters that make the observed data most likely. Fast, standard, gives a single best estimate. Your default.

MCMC — Bayesian sampling

Sample the full posterior distribution of the parameters. Gives uncertainty bounds and supports richer hierarchical models — but slower and heavier.

Heuristics & the finding

Heuristics are quick rules-of-thumb — fastest, least accurate. Simon (2025) showed the model-based methods (MLE / MCMC) beat heuristics for forecasting active customers and future purchases. That's why your slide lists all three and why benchmarking them is part of the plan.

The modern layer & your contribution

07Why add machine learning — and what's novel

BTYD is interpretable but rigid. ML is flexible but label-hungry. Your project sits in the gap between them.

Limits of pure BTYD

  • Assumes Poisson buying and one behaviour pattern for everyone.
  • Ignores covariates — it can't use age, channel, product category, promotions, etc.

What ML adds (and its catch)

  • XGBoost / gradient boosting, DNNs, GRU/LSTM use many features, capture non-linear and individual-level patterns.
  • The catch: they need churn labels (which non-contractual data lacks) and are far less interpretable.
Zero-inflation & hurdle models

Most customers make zero repeat purchases, so the target has a big spike at 0 that breaks ordinary regressors. A two-stage "hurdle" model (Lin, 2026) splits it: Stage 1 predicts will they buy at all?, Stage 2 predicts how much?

The research gap = your novelty

Non-contractual data has no churn labels, so supervised ML is stuck. BTYD's P(alive) is an unsupervised, probabilistic "soft label." Your contribution: (1) use P(alive) as that soft label; (2) unify BTYD's interpretable latent-churn inference with ML's feature richness in one pipeline; (3) make it value-aware by weighting churn risk with Gamma-Gamma CLV; (4) benchmark the estimation methods.

Defence

10Questions they'll ask — cover the answer and recite

Zeroth-review panels probe fundamentals and scope. These are the likely ones.

What exactly is non-contractual churn, and why can't you just label it?

There's no subscription or cancellation event, so no one records when a customer leaves — they simply stop buying. A long gap could be a dead customer or a dormant one. So there's no ground-truth churn label to train a classifier; we infer the churn probability instead.

Why the negative binomial and not a Poisson distribution?

A single customer's buying is Poisson, but customers have different rates. When you let the rate vary across customers with a Gamma distribution, the population's purchase counts become Negative Binomial. Real data is overdispersed (variance ≫ mean), which Poisson can't fit but NBD can.

Difference between Pareto/NBD and BG/NBD?

Same NBD buying process. They differ in dropout: Pareto/NBD lets a customer die at any continuous time (Pareto lifetimes); BG/NBD assumes dropout only happens just after a purchase (Beta-Geometric). BG/NBD has a closed-form likelihood, so it's simpler to fit with almost the same accuracy.

What is P(alive)?

The probability a customer is still active given their frequency, recency and age on file. It's the model's churn score — low P(alive) means likely churned. It comes out of the fitted model with no label required.

If there are no labels, how will you evaluate the model?

Hold out a future time window. Check calibration — do predicted purchase counts match what actually happened? For churn framed as a task, use the held-out period to define "did they return," then measure precision/recall/AUC. For value, compare predicted vs actual CLV with MAE/RMSE.

Then why not just use deep learning?

Deep models need labels we don't have and are hard to interpret. BTYD is interpretable and needs only RFM. Our plan uses BTYD's P(alive) as a soft label to enable ML, and benchmarks both — we get interpretability and flexibility rather than choosing one.

What's your dataset?

Public non-contractual retail transaction data — primarily the UCI Online Retail II dataset (invoice-level e-commerce), plus similar transaction logs. It's the standard benchmark used across our reference papers.

What is Gamma-Gamma for, and what does it assume?

It models the average monetary value per transaction so we can turn purchase counts into money (CLV). It assumes spend varies around a per-customer mean, those means are Gamma-distributed, and — importantly — that monetary value is independent of purchase frequency, which we verify with a correlation check.

What are the limitations of BTYD?

It assumes Poisson purchasing, treats customers as one behavioural type, and ignores covariates (product, channel, promotions). It also can't capture individual-level trend changes — which is exactly why we add an ML benchmark layer.

Hasn't this been done already?

BTYD and ML churn exist separately. Our contribution is the combination: using P(alive) as a soft churn label to bridge the missing-label problem, fusing probabilistic latent-churn inference with ML features in one pipeline, and prioritising retention by value (CLV), not churn risk alone.

What does the "T" in RFM+T mean?

T is the customer's age on file — the time from their first purchase to the end of the observation window. The model needs it to know how long the customer could have been buying, which is essential for judging whether a silence is meaningful.
