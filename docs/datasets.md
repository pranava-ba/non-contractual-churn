# Datasets & why we chose them

This project validates calibration on **real, public, non-contractual transaction logs** of
deliberately varied character, and on data simulated from the model itself. This page explains
what we use and — because it is the first question reviewers and readers ask — **why the age of
the data does not matter here, and why we deliberately avoid the newer "synthetic" datasets.**

## The benchmarks

| Dataset | Source & era | Cohort N | Character (active rate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CDNow** | US music e-tailer, 1997 | ~2,357 | sparse (25% active) — the field's canonical benchmark |
| **Grocery** | grocery, `BTYDplus` | ~1,525 | moderate (31%) |
| **Online Retail II** | UK gift retailer, 2009–2011 | ~4,268 | moderate e-commerce (48%) |
| **Olist** | Brazilian marketplace, 2016–2018 | ~22,597 | **extreme zero-inflation (1.6%)** |
| **Dunnhumby (Complete Journey)** | US grocery, ~2 yrs, + demographics | ~2,497 | **extreme density (96%)** |
| **Ta-Feng** | Taiwan grocery, 2000–2001 | ~22,715 | short-window grocery (65%) |
| **Simulated** | drawn from Pareto/NBD (and misspecified DGPs) | 100–2,000 | controlled ground truth |

The span from Olist's 1.6% active to Dunnhumby's 96% is the point: calibration is stress-tested
across the *entire* purchase-frequency spectrum, not just the narrow middle. Olist is an extreme
test of the zero-inflation coverage trap; Dunnhumby, of the dense/high-count regime.

## Why the age of the data doesn't matter

A natural worry is that CDNow (1997) or Online Retail (2010) are "too old." For a **calibration
and methodology** study they are not, for three concrete reasons:

1. **We test a property of the model, not a fact about a market.** The question is whether a
   model's *stated predictive uncertainty is honest* — whether its 95% intervals contain the
   truth 95% of the time. That property depends on the **structure** of purchasing (recency,
   frequency, and latent dropout), which is stable across decades and across industries. A 1997
   music buyer and a 2024 fashion buyer both generate recency/frequency signals with the same
   statistical shape; the calibration machinery neither knows nor cares what year it is.

2. **The field's benchmarks are old on purpose.** Real customer-level transaction logs are rarely
   released, for privacy reasons, so the shared public benchmarks (CDNow, Online Retail, Ta-Feng)
   are necessarily historical. Using the *same* benchmarks as the prior BTYD literature
   (Schmittlein, Fader–Hardie, Platzer–Reutterer, Simon) is a **feature, not a bug**: it makes our
   numbers directly comparable to that work. A brand-new private dataset would sacrifice exactly
   that comparability.

3. **We add breadth, not recency, where it helps.** Rather than chase a "recent" label, we widen
   the *behavioural* range (Olist's near-total zero-inflation, Dunnhumby's density), which is what
   actually exercises the calibration diagnostics. Recency of the calendar is irrelevant;
   diversity of behaviour is everything.

## Why we avoid newer "synthetic" datasets

Searching for post-2023 e-commerce datasets mostly turns up **synthetic** data ("a synthetic
dataset of 50k transactions…") or **cross-sectional, pre-aggregated RFM** tables. Both are
actively harmful for this study:

- **Synthetic data makes calibration circular.** Calibration asks whether a model's predictive
  distribution matches *reality*. If the "reality" was itself generated by some other random
  process, we would only be measuring how close our model is to *that generator's* assumptions —
  not to real customer behaviour. The result would be an artefact of the synthesiser, not a
  finding about the world. (Our *simulated* cohorts are used honestly for the opposite purpose:
  as a controlled ground truth where calibration is provable *by construction*, and clearly
  labelled as such.)

- **Cross-sectional RFM tables have no timeline.** BTYD models consume a *transaction event log*
  — the dates a customer purchased — to separate the live-purchasing process from latent dropout.
  A one-row-per-customer table of pre-computed recency/frequency/monetary values has thrown that
  timeline away, so it cannot be used to fit or fairly evaluate a buy-till-you-die model at all.

The newest *real* transaction-level option (H&M, 2022; data 2018–2020) was considered and set
aside: it adds scale and covariates but not validity that the existing spread lacks, and it does
not change any calibration conclusion. We would revisit it only for the covariate-specific
analysis, where its rich metadata would earn its place.

**In short:** we prefer old-but-real-and-comparable data over new-but-synthetic-or-aggregated
data, because calibration is a claim about reality, and only real transaction logs can test it.
