# Theoretical Foundation & Variance Decomposition

## Law of Total Variance

The central theoretical result explains why MCMC (full Bayesian sampling) and Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE plug-in) yield statistically equivalent predictive forecast distributions for customer purchase counts $x^*$.

By the law of total variance, the forecast predictive variance per customer $i$ decomposes into:

$$\mathrm{Var}(x^*_i \mid \text{data}) = \mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid \text{data}}\!\left[\mathrm{Var}(x^*_i \mid \theta, \text{data})\right] + \mathrm{Var}_{\theta \mid \text{data}}\!\left(\mathbb{E}[x^*_i \mid \theta, \text{data}]\right)$$

where $\theta = (r, \alpha, s, \beta)$ denotes the population heterogeneity parameters.

### Variance Order Analysis

1. **Individual Stochasticity ($\mathcal{O}(1)$)**:
   The first term represents individual-level stochasticity—the posterior uncertainty in $(\lambda_i, \mu_i, \tau_i)$ given customer $i$'s history $(x_i, t_{x,i}, T_i)$, compounded with Poisson count noise. This term is $\mathcal{O}(1)$ and does not shrink as the cohort size $N$ grows.

2. **Population Parameter Uncertainty ($\mathcal{O}(1/N)$)**:
   The second term represents population parameter uncertainty $\mathrm{Var}(\theta \mid \text{data})$. MCMC propagates this uncertainty while MLE holds $\theta$ fixed at $\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}$. By standard asymptotic normality of posterior distributions, this term contracts as $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$.

For any realistic cohort size ($N \ge 100$), the individual-level stochasticity completely dominates population parameter uncertainty. Consequently, the predictive distributions generated by MCMC and MLE coincide.

## Proper Scoring Rules & Calibration

- **Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS)**:
  $$\mathrm{CRPS}(F_i, y_i) = \mathbb{E}|X - y_i| - \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}|X - X'|$$
  Rewards calibration and sharpness jointly.

- **Randomized Probability Integral Transform (PIT)**:
  For discrete count forecasts $Y$, the randomized PIT is defined as:
  $$u_i = F_i(y_i - 1) + v_i [F_i(y_i) - F_i(y_i - 1)], \quad v_i \sim U(0,1)$$
  Under calibration, $u_i \sim U(0,1)$. Departures are measured via Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance (PIT-KS).

### Calibration metric: PIT-KS vs the planned PIT-KL (gap U3)

The gap analysis proposed an information-theoretic calibration score — the KL divergence of the empirical PIT histogram from $U(0,1)$ (its "Contribution #2"). The implementation instead reports the **Kolmogorov–Smirnov** distance of the PIT sample from uniform (PIT-KS) throughout. This was a deliberate substitution, not an oversight:

- **KS is binning-free.** KL of a histogram depends on an arbitrary bin count and is undefined (or inflated) in empty bins without ad-hoc smoothing; KS uses the raw empirical CDF, so there is no bin-width knob to tune.
- **KS has a known, testable null.** It pairs directly with the **Lilliefors bootstrap correction** already implemented (`src/run_pit_bootstrap.py`) for the estimated-parameter case; there is no comparably standard finite-sample null for a histogram-KL.
- **KS is the field-standard PIT diagnostic** in the forecasting literature (Czado, Gneiting & Held 2009), keeping the results comparable to prior work.

KL and KS agree on the qualitative verdict — both vanish iff the PIT is uniform — so nothing in the substantive findings depends on the choice. Where an information-theoretic summary is specifically wanted, the KL of a smoothed PIT histogram can be reported as a secondary score, but it adds no inferential power over the bootstrap-calibrated KS.

## Zero-Inflation Asymmetry: Median-of-Draws vs Closed-Form Mean (gap T1)

Simon (2025) observes that the MCMC per-customer point forecast can be **exactly zero** for likely-dropped-out customers, while the MLE closed-form forecast is always strictly positive, and reads this as an advantage of MCMC. The asymmetry is real but is **not** a Bayesian-vs-frequentist effect — it is the difference between two *summary functionals* (a median and a mean) applied to essentially the same zero-inflated predictive law.

### Setup

Conditional on individual parameters $(\lambda_i, \mu_i)$ and the calibration history, the forecast target is

$$x^*_i \mid \lambda_i, \mu_i, \tau_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_i L_i), \qquad L_i = \max\{0,\ \min(\tau_i, T+T^*) - T\},$$

where $\tau_i$ is the latent dropout time. The predictive of $x^*_i$ is **zero-inflated**: any customer who has dropped out by $T$ contributes an *exact* zero ($L_i = 0$), and even an alive customer contributes Poisson mass $e^{-\lambda_i L_i}$ at zero, so $\Pr(x^*_i = 0 \mid \lambda_i,\mu_i) \ge \Pr(\tau_i \le T) > 0$.

### The two forecasts are two functionals of this law

**MLE closed form — a mean.** The Schmittlein et al. (1987) conditional expectation is

$$\widehat{x}^{*,\mathrm{MLE}}_i = \mathbb{E}[x^*_i \mid \text{data}, \hat\theta] = \iint \mathbb{E}[x^*_i \mid \lambda,\mu]\; p(\lambda,\mu \mid \text{data}, \hat\theta)\, d\lambda\, d\mu \; > \; 0,$$

strictly positive because it integrates a positive integrand over a posterior with full support on $(0,\infty)^2$ — the mean of a nonnegative variable with any positive-support mass is strictly positive.

**MCMC — a median of draws.** The MCMC point forecast is the median of posterior-predictive draws $x^{*(j)}_i = \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_i^{(j)} L_i^{(j)})$ with $\tau_i^{(j)}$ **sampled**. Whenever $\tau_i^{(j)} \le T$ the draw is an exact zero, and

$$\mathrm{median}_j\{x^{*(j)}_i\} = 0 \iff \#\{j : x^{*(j)}_i = 0\} > J/2 \iff \Pr(x^*_i = 0 \mid \text{data}) > \tfrac{1}{2},$$

i.e. the MCMC point forecast is exactly zero precisely for the customers whose predictive puts majority mass on zero.

### Reconciliation

A **mean** and a **median** of a zero-inflated law differ whenever more than half the mass sits at zero — there the median is $0$ while the mean is positive. The estimation paradigm does not create the zeros:

- Summarise the **MLE plug-in** predictive (individual draws at fixed $\hat\theta$, `score.conditional_individual_draws`) by its *median* and it too returns exact zeros.
- Summarise the **MCMC** predictive by its *mean* (posterior-predictive mean) and it is strictly positive.

So "MCMC assigns exact zeros, MLE cannot" pairs MCMC-*median* against MLE-*mean*; under a common functional the two agree. This is exactly what the law-of-total-variance result predicts (the two predictive laws coincide up to the $O(1/N)$ parameter term) and is why the distribution-level comparison finds **MCMC ≈ MLE** rather than an MCMC advantage. Zero-inflation is a property of *reporting a median of a zero-inflated forecast* — available to either estimator — not a distinguishing capability of MCMC.

## Empirical Noise-Floor Decomposition (gap T3)

The law of total variance splits the predictive variance into an $O(1)$ individual term and an $O(1/N)$ parameter term. On simulated data — where the ground-truth parameters are known — we can *measure* the corresponding split of forecast **error** by scoring the same target three ways (`src/run_noise_floor.py`):

1. **est** — predictive at the MLE estimate $\hat\theta$ (total error; what a practitioner obtains);
2. **true_hyper** — predictive at the *true* hyperparameters $(r,\alpha,s,\beta)$ (removes estimation error);
3. **true_indiv** — predictive using each customer's *true* $(\lambda_i,\mu_i,\tau_i)$ (only Poisson counting noise remains).

Then

$$\underbrace{\mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{est}}}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{(\mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{est}} - \mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{true\_hyper}})}_{\text{fixable: estimation, } O(1/N)} + \underbrace{(\mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{true\_hyper}} - \mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{true\_indiv}})}_{\text{individual posterior, } O(1)} + \underbrace{\mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{true\_indiv}}}_{\text{counting-noise floor}}.$$

Result (mean CRPS over 6 seeds, horizon $T^*=26$ weeks, regime $E(\lambda)=0.15$, $E(\mu)=0.08$):

| $N$ | total (est) | true_hyper | true_indiv | fixable | individual | floor |
|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| 500 | 0.383 | 0.383 | 0.218 | 0.000 | 0.165 | 0.218 |
| 1000 | 0.386 | 0.387 | 0.219 | −0.001 | 0.168 | 0.219 |
| 2000 | 0.416 | 0.417 | 0.231 | −0.001 | 0.186 | 0.231 |
| 4000 | 0.404 | 0.404 | 0.225 | 0.000 | 0.179 | 0.225 |

The **fixable (estimation) share is $\approx 0$ even at $N=500$** — `est` and `true_hyper` are indistinguishable (the small negatives are Monte-Carlo noise). Essentially all forecast error is the counting-noise floor (~56% of CRPS) plus the individual-posterior term (~44%); **none is hyperparameter estimation error**.

This is the empirical face of the MCMC ≈ MLE null: you cannot forecast individual purchase counts better by estimating $(r,\alpha,s,\beta)$ more precisely — the ceiling is set by individual-level and counting stochasticity, which no estimator and no amount of calibration data removes. It also bounds the achievable gain from the estimation-side extensions (E1 alternative samplers, E4 approximate Bayes): there is essentially no estimation error left to recover.
